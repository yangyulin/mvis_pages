Use this page to see which motion makes which calibration value measurable, and
why.

A value is **observable** when the measurements change if the value changes. If
they do not, the data cannot tell a right value from a wrong one, however long
you record. The solver then returns roughly its start value, with a wide 1σ.

Whether a calibration value is observable depends on the motion. The MVIS paper
analyses this for the full multi-camera, multi-IMU state, and the earlier
papers below give the degenerate motions for the time offset, extrinsics and IMU
intrinsics:

- Y. Yang, P. Geneva, G. Huang,
  [Multi-visual-inertial system: Analysis, calibration, and estimation](https://doi.org/10.1177/02783649241245726),
  The International Journal of Robotics Research 43(13), pp. 1995–2026, 2024.
- Y. Yang, P. Geneva, K. Eckenhoff, G. Huang,
  [Degenerate motion analysis for aided INS with online spatial and temporal sensor calibration](https://doi.org/10.1109/LRA.2019.2893803),
  IEEE Robotics and Automation Letters 4(2), pp. 2070–2077, 2019.
- Y. Yang, P. Geneva, X. Zuo, G. Huang,
  [Online IMU intrinsic calibration: Is it necessary?](https://doi.org/10.15607/RSS.2020.XVI.026),
  Robotics: Science and Systems XVI, 2020.

This page gives the short reason for each value. Symbols follow
[Notation & glossary](/concepts/notation/); the factors are on
[Camera–IMU calibration math](/concepts/camera-imu-math/).

## Never observable: four directions

The measurements are relative: the cameras see the board, the IMUs feel motion
and gravity. Moving the whole scene (board and trajectory) or turning it about
gravity changes nothing they measure. So four directions are never observable:

- **global position** (3);
- **rotation about gravity** (1).

MVIS holds them with a prior on the first state's position and its rotation
about gravity. The tilt (the direction of gravity in the IMU frame) stays free:
the accelerometer sees gravity, so it is observable. Metric
scale is observable too: the board has a known size, and the accelerometer
measures in m/s².

## What each value needs

| Value | Needs | Short reason |
|---|---|---|
| Camera intrinsics $\mathbf{f}_i$, $\mathbf{d}_i$ | the board seen at many distances, angles and across the whole image | from the board alone |
| Extrinsic rotation ${}^{I}_{C_i}\mathbf{R}$ | rotation about at least 2 axes | camera and gyro see the same angular velocity, rotated |
| Lever arm ${}^{I}\mathbf{p}_{C_i}$ | rotation about at least 2 axes, with changing angular velocity | it shows up only through rotation |
| Time offset $t_{d,i}$ | angular velocity that changes, and some translation | a time shift moves a pose by its velocity |
| Readout $t_{r,i}$ | fast rotation about the camera's x or y axis, board spread over many rows | each row is shifted by its own share of the readout |
| Gyro scale and misalignment $\mathbf{D}_w$ | rotation about every axis, at varied rates | each column multiplies one axis of the angular velocity |
| Accel scale and misalignment $\mathbf{D}_a$, bias $\mathbf{b}_a$ | gravity along every axis (tilts), plus linear acceleration changes | each column multiplies one axis of the specific force |
| G-sensitivity $\mathbf{T}_g$ | strong linear acceleration while rotating on all axes | the gyro error must vary with acceleration, apart from rotation |
| `t_ga` | quickly changing acceleration (brisk reversals) | it enters only through the rate of change of acceleration |
| Auxiliary IMU rotation and $t_{d,j}$ | rotation about at least 2 axes | the two gyros see the same angular velocity |
| Auxiliary IMU lever arm | rotation about at least 2 axes, with changing angular velocity | centripetal and tangential acceleration |

The motion in [step 2: record the dataset](/guides/calibration-motion/) is built
to cover every row of this table.

## The reasons, in equations

### Extrinsic rotation: two directions needed

The camera's angular velocity (from its board poses) and the gyro's are the same
vector in two frames:

$$
{}^{I}\boldsymbol\omega(t) = {}^{I}_{C_i}\mathbf{R}\;{}^{C_i}\boldsymbol\omega(t) \quad\text{(after time alignment)}
$$

A rotation is fixed by two non-parallel vector pairs. If every
${}^{C_i}\boldsymbol\omega$ is parallel to one axis, any rotation about that axis
fits equally well. The start-value step of MVIS records the singular values of
the camera–gyro angular-velocity cross-covariance for this reason: one large and
two tiny ones mean one-axis rotation.

### Lever arm: only rotation shows it

The camera sits at ${}^{G}\mathbf{p}_{C} = {}^{G}\mathbf{p}_{I} + {}^{G}_{I}\mathbf{R}\,{}^{I}\mathbf{p}_{C}$.
Differentiate twice and compare with what the accelerometer measures:

$$
{}^{G}\ddot{\mathbf{p}}_{C} = {}^{G}\ddot{\mathbf{p}}_{I} +
{}^{G}_{I}\mathbf{R}\Big(\big[{}^{I}\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}\big]_\times + \big[{}^{I}\boldsymbol\omega\big]_\times^2\Big)\,{}^{I}\mathbf{p}_{C}
$$

- **No rotation:** both terms vanish and ${}^{I}\mathbf{p}_{C}$ is a constant
  offset the board pose absorbs.
- **Rotation about one axis $\mathbf{k}$:** a component of ${}^{I}\mathbf{p}_{C}$
  along $\mathbf{k}$ gives $\mathbf{k}\times\mathbf{p} = 0$, so that component
  stays unobservable.

The same holds for the lever arm of an auxiliary IMU.

### Time offset: a shift is seen through velocity

A time offset error $\delta t$ moves the pose used for a frame to a slightly
different time:

$$
{}^{G}_{I}\mathbf{T}(t + \delta t) \approx {}^{G}_{I}\mathbf{T}(t)\;\mathrm{Exp}\!\left(\begin{bmatrix}{}^{I}\boldsymbol\omega\\ {}^{I}\mathbf{v}\end{bmatrix}\delta t\right)
$$

- **At rest** the shift changes nothing.
- **At constant angular and linear velocity** the shift is a constant relative
  transform. The extrinsic absorbs it, and $t_{d,i}$ and the extrinsic cannot be
  told apart.
- **When the velocity changes** the shift varies over the recording and no
  constant transform can explain it. That is the signal.

### Readout: a shift per row

On a rolling-shutter camera, a corner on row $v_m$ is captured at
$t_C + t_{d,i} + (v_m/H)\,t_{r,i}$. The readout is a time offset that grows from
the top row to the bottom row. It needs motion that moves the rows differently:
rotation about the camera's x or y axis, which sweeps the image vertically or
horizontally. Rotation about the optical axis gives little signal, and so does a
board that covers only a few rows: then $t_{r,i}$ cannot be told from $t_{d,i}$.
See [Why calibrate rolling shutter](/why/rolling-shutter/).

### IMU scale and misalignment: every axis excited

The corrected readings are
${}^{I}\boldsymbol\omega = {}^{I}_{w}\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{D}_w(\boldsymbol\omega_m - \mathbf{b}_g - \mathbf{T}_g\,{}^{I}\mathbf{a})$
and ${}^{I}\mathbf{a} = {}^{I}_{a}\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{D}_a(\mathbf{a}_m - \mathbf{b}_a)$
([Base IMU factor](/concepts/camera-imu-math/base-imu/#corrected-readings)).
Column $n$ of $\mathbf{D}_w$ multiplies only axis $n$ of the reading. If that axis
never turns, the column has nothing to scale.

The accelerometer measures specific force, ${}^{I}\mathbf{a} = {}^{I}_{G}\mathbf{R}\,({}^{G}\ddot{\mathbf{p}}_I - {}^{G}\mathbf{g})$.
Gravity is about 9.8 m/s², far larger than hand-held acceleration. So tilting the
rig until each axis has pointed along gravity is what excites $\mathbf{D}_a$. A
rig held at one tilt sees one fixed gravity vector: then the scale and the bias
along it trade off against each other.

### G-sensitivity: acceleration must vary apart from rotation

$\mathbf{T}_g\,{}^{I}\mathbf{a}$ adds to the gyro reading. With mostly gravity in
${}^{I}\mathbf{a}$, its effect looks like a gyro bias that changes with tilt, which
the biases and $\mathbf{D}_w$ can also explain. It separates only with strong
linear acceleration on all axes. Hand-held motion rarely gives that, so it stays
pinned by default.

### `t_ga`: seen through the change in acceleration

The accelerometer reading that belongs to gyro time $t$ is the one stamped
$t + t_{ga}$. To first order:

$$
\mathbf{a}_m(t + t_{ga}) \approx \mathbf{a}_m(t) + t_{ga}\,\dot{\mathbf{a}}_m(t)
$$

The offset is visible only through $\dot{\mathbf{a}}_m$, the rate of change of
the specific force. Smooth, steady motion leaves it loose; brisk reversals make it
sharp. The response of some IMUs also depends on the frequency of the motion, so
one value can be a compromise: see [Why calibrate t_ga](/why/tga/).

## How much signal is enough

Observable is not yes or no in practice. A value can be observable in theory and
still weak on a given recording: the 1σ then comes out wide, and the value moves
from one recording to the next. Two ways to check:

- **Read the 1σ** next to each value in the result. Compare it across recordings.
- **Run the digital twin** of your own rig and motion: MVIS simulates the
  recording with known truth and solves it ([step 7](/guides/simulation/)).

What happens when a motion is missing, and how MVIS reports it:
[Degeneracy](/concepts/degeneracy/).

## What this means for your recording

- **Rotate about all three axes**, at varied speed, with the board in view.
- **Tilt the rig through many orientations**, so gravity falls along every axis.
- **Change direction briskly** a few times. Avoid long stretches of steady motion.
- **Sweep the board across all image rows**, more so for a rolling-shutter camera.
- **Hold still for about 3 s at both ends.** The rests give the motion a clean
  start and end; the start-value step can compare a still start with its
  gyro-bias estimate.
